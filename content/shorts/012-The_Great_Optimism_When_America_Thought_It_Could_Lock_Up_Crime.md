Small rural jail in Buck Grove, Iowa, being repurposed as farm building in 1919

In the autumn of 1919, the good people of Buck Grove, Iowa, made a decision that would seem laughably naive in hindsight. They sold their jail. Not to convert it into a museum or community center, mind you, but to a local farmer who promptly transformed the modest structure into something far more practical for rural life: a combination pig pen and chicken coop. The townspeople, all 188 of them, figured they simply wouldn't need it anymore. After all, prohibition was coming, and with it, the end of crime in America.

This wasn't the fevered delusion of a single small town. It was the crystallization of a nationwide belief so profound, so deeply held, that it seemed not just reasonable but inevitable. From coast to coast, Americans had convinced themselves that alcohol was the root of virtually every social ill, and that eliminating it would usher in an era of unprecedented peace and prosperity. The jail in Buck Grove wasn't an anomaly—it was a symbol of an entire nation's breathtaking optimism about human nature and the power of moral reform.

## The Making of a Moral Crusade

To understand how an entire country could reach such a conclusion, you have to go back to the early 19th century, when America was undergoing a profound spiritual awakening. The Second Great Awakening, that great wave of evangelical fervor that swept across the young republic, carried with it a revolutionary idea: that society could be perfected through moral reform[^1]. This wasn't just about individual salvation anymore—it was about creating Heaven on Earth.

The movement's leaders, figures like Charles Grandison Finney, preached something called "perfectionism"[^1]. The concept was both simple and audacious: truly redeemed Christians could live free of sin and, by their example, transform the world around them. It was spiritual bootstrap theology with a social mission, and it gave birth to an entire constellation of reform movements. Prison reform, education reform, women's rights, and most significantly, temperance—all were seen as interconnected parts of humanity's march toward moral perfection[^2].

The temperance movement found its voice in preachers like Lyman Beecher, who delivered six thunderous sermons in 1825 that would be published the following year as "Six Sermons on the Nature, Occasions, Signs, Evils, and Remedy of Intemperance"[^1]. Beecher didn't just condemn alcohol—he painted it as the great destroyer, the single thread that, when pulled, would unravel the entire tapestry of American virtue.

This message resonated deeply with a middle class anxious about the rapid changes transforming their country. The Market Revolution was creating new fortunes and new forms of poverty, new opportunities and new temptations. Cities were growing, immigrants were arriving, and traditional social hierarchies were shifting[^3]. In this environment of uncertainty, the temperance movement offered something precious: a simple explanation for complex problems and an achievable solution.

## The Birth of Billy Sunday's America

By the early 20th century, the temperance cause had found its most charismatic champion in Billy Sunday, a former professional baseball player turned evangelical preacher[^4]. Sunday was temperance theater at its most dramatic—part revival meeting, part vaudeville show, part political rally. His famous "Booze Sermon" became the movement's rallying cry, delivered to millions across dozens of cities[^5].

Sunday's message was uncompromising: alcohol was "God's worst enemy" and "hell's best friend"[^6]. But more than that, he preached that prohibition would transform America into a kind of earthly paradise. "The reign of tears is over," he proclaimed in one of his most famous sermons. "The slums will soon be only a memory. We will turn our prisons into factories and our jails into storehouses and corncribs. Men will walk upright now, women will smile, and the children will laugh. Hell will be forever for rent"[^7][^8].

This wasn't metaphorical language—Sunday and his followers genuinely believed that eliminating alcohol would eliminate crime. The logic seemed unassailable: temperance advocates had been documenting the connection between alcohol and antisocial behavior for decades. Prison interviews consistently showed inmates claiming their crimes stemmed from drink[^8]. Police reports linked alcohol to domestic violence, public disorder, and petty crime. If you removed the cause, surely you'd eliminate the effect.

When the Eighteenth Amendment finally passed in 1919, prohibitionists felt vindicated. They had convinced not just a majority, but a constitutional supermajority that their diagnosis was correct[^9]. The Anti-Saloon League, the movement's most effective political organization, celebrated with elaborate pageantry. Across the country, mock funerals were held for "John Barleycorn," the personification of alcohol[^6]. In Norfolk, Virginia, Billy Sunday presided over one such ceremony before 10,000 people, complete with a 20-foot-long coffin and a man dressed as the devil serving as chief mourner[^10].

In Chicago, the Second United Presbyterian Church held its own elaborate funeral, featuring a six-foot-tall bottle placed upside down in a coffin—"so that if he ever wriggles away he will wriggle in the right direction," the minister explained[^10]. The bottle was then embalmed, cremated, and buried to the applause of the dry-eyed crowd.

## The Optimism of Reform

The decision to sell jails wasn't just about alcohol—it was about a broader faith in the power of institutional reform to solve social problems. The same period that produced prohibition also produced the modern American prison system, built on the revolutionary idea that criminals could be rehabilitated rather than simply punished[^11].

The Auburn System, developed in New York, and the Pennsylvania System, pioneered in Philadelphia, both reflected this optimistic belief in human perfectibility[^12]. Prison reformers genuinely believed they were creating institutions that would not just house criminals but transform them into productive citizens. The very word "penitentiary" reflected this hope—it was a place where offenders would become penitent, where they would find redemption through reflection and labor.

This reformist optimism wasn't confined to prisons. Americans of the antebellum period launched campaigns to improve conditions in mental asylums, establish public schools, care for orphans, and protect the mentally ill[^2]. They created utopian communities, founded colleges, and launched newspapers, all driven by an unshakeable faith that social problems could be identified and solved through organized effort[^13].

The temperance movement fit perfectly into this framework. It offered a comprehensive theory of social dysfunction and a clear path to improvement. More importantly, it promised that moral suasion—the power of persuasion and example—could achieve what force alone could not[^14].

## The Rude Awakening

The reality of prohibition, of course, was far different from the promise. Within months of the Volstead Act taking effect, it became clear that eliminating legal alcohol had not eliminated the demand for it. Instead, it had created a massive black market dominated by increasingly sophisticated criminal organizations[^15].

The statistics were sobering. Rather than emptying prisons, prohibition filled them to overflowing. A study of thirty major U.S. cities found that crime increased by 24% between 1920 and 1921[^8]. Thefts and burglaries rose by 9%, homicides by 13%, and assaults by 13%[^8]. The national homicide rate, which had been declining steadily, reversed course and climbed throughout the 1920s[^8].

Federal prisons, which housed 5,268 long-term inmates in 1921, held 14,115 by 1930—a nearly threefold increase[^16]. By 1930, over one-third of federal prisoners were there for violations of the National Prohibition Act and other liquor laws[^17]. Prison overcrowding became so severe that inmates were sleeping on cots in corridors, multiple prisoners were crammed into cells designed for one, and some facilities were forced to keep prisoners locked up for 20 hours a day due to lack of space[^17].

The Wickersham Commission, appointed by President Herbert Hoover to study the enforcement of prohibition, painted a grim picture. The federal courts, the commission reported, were "ill adapted" to enforcing prohibition and had been overwhelmed by the sheer volume of cases[^18]. The commission noted that more than eight-ninths of convictions were the result of guilty pleas, often accompanied by light sentences that robbed the law of its deterrent effect[^18]. The volume of cases, the report concluded, had "injured their dignity, impaired their efficiency, and endangered the wholesome respect for them that once obtained"[^18].

## The Deeper Lessons

The story of Buck Grove's jail—and the broader optimism it represented—offers profound insights into the American character and the nature of social reform. The prohibitionists weren't wrong to identify alcohol as a significant social problem. Rates of alcohol consumption in the early 19th century were genuinely alarming, and the temperance movement's early phase, which relied on moral persuasion rather than legal prohibition, achieved remarkable success in reducing drinking[^14].

But the movement's ultimate failure lay in its misunderstanding of human nature and social systems. The prohibitionists made the classic mistake of confusing correlation with causation. Yes, alcohol was present in many crimes and social problems, but it was often a symptom rather than a cause of deeper issues like poverty, inequality, and social dislocation[^8].

More fundamentally, the prohibition experiment revealed the limits of using law to regulate personal behavior, especially when that behavior is deeply embedded in cultural practices and social networks[^14]. The temperance movement had achieved its greatest successes when it changed hearts and minds; it failed catastrophically when it tried to change behavior through coercion alone.

The broader reform movements of the period suffered from similar misapprehensions about the malleability of human nature and social institutions. Prison reform, while it eliminated some of the worst abuses of earlier systems, didn't achieve the transformation of criminals that its advocates had promised[^12]. Many of the utopian communities founded in the period collapsed within a few years[^13]. The various moral reform movements, while they accomplished significant good, often fell short of their grandiose promises.

## The Enduring Appeal of Simple Solutions

What's perhaps most striking about the prohibition era is how reasonable the initial premise seemed to so many intelligent, well-meaning people. The Anti-Saloon League wasn't a fringe organization—it was one of the most effective political movements in American history, capable of mobilizing millions of voters and passing a constitutional amendment[^19]. Billy Sunday wasn't a crank preacher—he was one of the most popular speakers of his era, drawing crowds that would make modern evangelists envious[^5].

The appeal of their message lay partly in its comprehensiveness. The temperance movement didn't just promise to reduce crime—it promised to eliminate poverty, strengthen families, improve public health, and restore moral order[^8]. It offered a single solution to multiple problems, a master key to unlock all of society's doors.

This kind of thinking has deep roots in American culture. The country was founded on the radical proposition that human institutions could be consciously designed and improved, that the past didn't have to dictate the future[^11]. The same optimistic spirit that produced the Constitution, the Bill of Rights, and the Declaration of Independence also produced prohibition and the confident belief that crime could be legislated out of existence.

That optimism has both inspired America's greatest achievements and led to some of its most spectacular failures. The civil rights movement succeeded because it combined moral force with practical politics and a deep understanding of human psychology[^14]. The War on Poverty achieved significant reductions in want and privation, even if it didn't eliminate poverty entirely. But other reform efforts—from urban renewal to the war on drugs—have faltered when they confused good intentions with effective policy.

## The Aftermath

By 1933, when the Twenty-First Amendment repealed prohibition, the experiment had been thoroughly discredited[^20]. Crime rates plummeted almost immediately after repeal, providing stark evidence of the policy's counterproductive effects[^8]. The criminal organizations that had grown rich on bootlegging either disbanded or turned to other activities. The federal courts, freed from the burden of processing endless liquor cases, regained their efficiency and dignity.

Buck Grove, Iowa, meanwhile, continued its quiet existence. By the time prohibition ended, the town's population had dwindled to just over 100 people. The jail-turned-chicken-coop presumably continued serving its new purpose, a humble reminder of a time when America thought it could perfect itself through legislation.

Today, Buck Grove has just 34 residents[^21], fewer than the number of comments on some Reddit posts about its brief moment of historical notoriety. The town that once symbolized America's boundless faith in moral reform has become a symbol of rural decline, a reminder that social forces are often more complex and persistent than any single policy intervention can address.

## The Enduring Questions

The story of prohibition raises questions that remain relevant today. How do we distinguish between problems that require legal solutions and those that require cultural change? When does legitimate concern about social problems tip over into moral panic? How do we maintain healthy skepticism about simple solutions while still believing in the possibility of meaningful reform?

Perhaps most importantly, the prohibition era reminds us that good intentions are not enough. The prohibitionists were sincere in their desire to reduce human suffering and create a better society. Their methods were democratic—they convinced majorities in state after state to support their cause. Their opponents were often unsavory—the liquor industry did profit from human weakness and often allied itself with corrupt political machines.

But sincerity, democracy, and moral authority aren't sufficient to guarantee policy success. Effective reform requires not just good intentions but accurate diagnosis of problems, realistic assessment of solutions, and humble recognition of the limits of what law and government can achieve.

The jail in Buck Grove stands—or stood—as a monument to a particular kind of American optimism, the belief that social problems are puzzles to be solved rather than tensions to be managed. It's an optimism that has driven much of what's best about American society, from the abolition of slavery to the expansion of civil rights to the creation of social safety nets. But it's also an optimism that can lead to overreach when it loses touch with the complexities of human nature and social systems.

The prohibitionists were wrong about alcohol being the root of all evil, but they were right about something else: society can be changed, improved, and reformed. The trick is knowing the difference between problems that can be solved and problems that can only be ameliorated, between reforms that work with human nature and those that work against it. The people of Buck Grove learned that distinction the hard way, as did the rest of America. The question is whether we've learned it well enough to avoid making similar mistakes in the future.

In the end, the empty jail in Buck Grove wasn't just a symbol of misplaced optimism—it was a reminder that in America, even the most spectacular failures often begin with the noblest intentions. And perhaps that's not entirely a bad thing. A society that occasionally overestimates its capacity for self-improvement is probably healthier than one that never tries to improve at all. The challenge is learning to channel that optimism wisely, to reform without destroying, to hope without forgetting the lessons of history.

The chickens and pigs that eventually occupied that converted jail probably gave little thought to the grand dreams that once led to its construction. But their presence there tells its own story about American pragmatism—the ability to adapt, improvise, and find new uses for old ideas when the original plans don't quite work out.
