In the spring of 2013, outside the Sacramento County Jail, a small figure waited patiently among the usual assemblage of visitors, lawyers, and hangers-on. Etta Mae Lopez, thirty-one years old and five feet one inch tall, had stationed herself there for hours with a plan that would have seemed, to most observers, both desperate and absurd[^1][^2]. She was waiting for a sheriff's deputy to emerge so that she could slap him across the face. Her goal was not revenge, nor was it some misguided attempt at martyrdom. It was, quite simply, to get herself arrested and sentenced to jail, where California's tobacco ban would force her to quit smoking[^3][^4].

When Deputy Matt Campoy finished his shift and walked through the jail's exit, Lopez stepped directly into his path. Without preamble or explanation, she struck him across the face[^1][^2]. The deputy, startled but professional, immediately arrested her. It was only then, as he processed her into custody, that Lopez revealed the peculiar logic behind her assault. "She told us that she needed to quit smoking," Campoy later recounted to reporters, adding with the sort of gallows humor that law enforcement develops over years of witnessing human folly, "I've been telling everyone I have a new Irish name: Nick O'Derm"[^3][^2].

The story might have ended there, filed away as another eccentric footnote in the annals of petty crime, were it not for what it revealed about the complicated relationship between addiction, desperation, and America's carceral system. Lopez's case, which resulted in a sixty-three-day jail sentence after she pleaded no contest to misdemeanor battery of a peace officer[^3][^5], illuminated the peculiar ways in which prison tobacco bans—ostensibly health measures—had created an unexpected form of involuntary rehabilitation.

California had implemented its comprehensive prison smoking ban in 2005, following legislation that was promoted as both a fiscal and health necessity[^6][^7]. The ban was part of a broader national trend; by 2013, seventeen states had completely prohibited tobacco use in their correctional facilities, while thirty-one others maintained partial bans[^8]. The rationale was compelling: with smoking rates among prisoners hovering around seventy-five to ninety percent—far higher than the general population—the health care costs were staggering[^8][^9]. Assemblyman Tim Leslie, the Republican who championed California's legislation, predicted savings of at least two hundred and sixty-five million dollars annually[^7].

But the reality of tobacco prohibition behind bars proved more complex than the sanitized projections of cost savings suggested. At institutions like Folsom State Prison, where tobacco had been banned even before the statewide prohibition, a thriving black market had emerged almost immediately[^7]. A tin of Bugler tobacco that once sold for eleven dollars in the prison commissary commanded two hundred dollars on the cellblock. Cigarette papers, lighters, even matches became precious commodities, traded with the intensity of wartime rationing[^7]. More troubling still, the prohibition had spawned what one observer described as "a network of tobacco brokers, middlemen and enforcers" assigned to collect debts from desperate smokers[^7].

The violence that accompanied these underground tobacco economies was predictable and swift. When Maine implemented its prison smoking ban in 2000, assaults quadrupled[^7][^10]. The pattern repeated itself across the country: forced abstinence did not eliminate the craving for nicotine, it merely drove the trade underground, where disputes were settled not through civil courts but through the brutal calculus of prison justice[^11].

For Etta Lopez, however, the coercive environment of a tobacco-free jail represented not a source of violence but a solution to a problem she felt powerless to solve on her own. Her neighbors in Sacramento, who knew her as a heavy smoker, were baffled by her strategy. "There's easier ways to stop smoking than hitting a cop," one told reporters. "No, that's not the way I want to quit"[^2][^4]. Anti-smoking advocates were similarly perplexed. Kimberly Bankston-Lee from Breathe California observed that if someone felt compelled to assault a police officer in order to quit smoking, "that lets us know in the community that we have a real problem"[^2][^4].

Yet Lopez's approach, however extreme, reflected a deeper understanding of the psychology of addiction than many conventional cessation programs. Research has consistently shown that environmental controls are among the most effective tools for breaking habitual behaviors[^9][^12]. The controlled environment of a jail, with its rigid schedules, limited freedoms, and absolute prohibition on tobacco, created what behavioral scientists call "stimulus control"—the removal of environmental cues that trigger addictive behaviors.

The irony was not lost on corrections officials, who found themselves inadvertently running what amounted to large-scale smoking cessation programs. Studies of prison tobacco bans showed that while they were effective in reducing smoking-related health problems during incarceration—respiratory ailments dropped dramatically at facilities like the California Men's Colony—the long-term success rates were dismally low[^6][^9]. Research indicated that ninety-eight percent of prisoners who were forced to quit smoking while incarcerated relapsed within days of their release[^9][^13]. The absence of follow-up support, combined with the stress of reintegration into society, made the transition back to smoking almost inevitable.

This pattern of relapse pointed to a fundamental flaw in using incarceration as a smoking cessation tool. While the controlled environment of a jail or prison could effectively interrupt the physical habit of smoking, it did little to address the underlying psychological and social factors that drove tobacco use. For many inmates, cigarettes served multiple functions: they were currency in an economy of scarcity, a social lubricant in a hostile environment, and a rare source of autonomy in a system designed to eliminate personal choice[^11][^14]. The boredom, stress, and isolation of prison life—factors that Lopez presumably hoped to use to her advantage—were, paradoxically, some of the same conditions that made smoking so appealing to inmates in the first place[^12][^15].

The broader context of Lopez's story reveals the peculiar ways in which America's mass incarceration system has become entangled with public health policy. Prisons and jails, originally designed for punishment and rehabilitation, have increasingly found themselves serving as de facto mental health facilities, drug treatment centers, and now, smoking cessation clinics. The overlap is not entirely accidental: the populations most likely to be incarcerated—poor, undereducated, disproportionately minority communities—are also those with the highest smoking rates and the least access to conventional health care[^13][^16].

This intersection of criminal justice and public health created a kind of perverse logic where individuals like Lopez could rationally conclude that committing a crime was their best path to medical intervention. The fact that such a calculation could make sense to someone speaks to broader failures in America's health care system, particularly around addiction treatment. Effective smoking cessation programs—combining behavioral therapy, pharmacological interventions, and long-term support—remain expensive and often inaccessible to those who need them most[^14][^12].

Lopez's sixty-three-day sentence represented a period long enough to break the physical addiction to nicotine, which typically requires three to four weeks. Whether it was sufficient to address the behavioral and psychological components of her addiction was another question entirely. The research on prison-based smoking cessation suggests that without continued support after release, the vast majority of individuals return to their previous smoking patterns within days[^9][^13].

The deputy she assaulted, Matt Campoy, seemed to understand this complexity better than most. His joke about adopting the name "Nick O'Derm"—a reference to the nicotine patch brand NicoDerm—revealed not just gallows humor but perhaps a recognition that he had become an unwilling participant in someone else's desperate attempt at self-medication[^3][^2]. His role in Lopez's story was purely incidental, yet it highlighted the ways in which the criminal justice system had become a catch-all solution for problems that society had failed to address through other means.

The case of Etta Lopez represents more than just an amusing anecdote about unconventional problem-solving. It is a stark illustration of how the intersection of addiction, poverty, and mass incarceration creates situations where rational people make seemingly irrational choices. Her decision to assault a police officer in order to quit smoking was, within the constrained logic of her circumstances, perfectly reasonable. She had identified a problem—her inability to quit smoking—and devised a solution that used the coercive power of the state to enforce the behavioral change she felt incapable of achieving on her own[^1][^3][^2].

Whether her plan ultimately succeeded remains unknown. The media attention surrounding her case focused on the novelty of her approach rather than its long-term effectiveness. Like most stories that capture public attention for their oddity, Lopez's tale was quickly forgotten, leaving unanswered the question of whether her sixty-three days in Sacramento County Jail accomplished what years of personal willpower had not.

What remains clear is that her story serves as an unwitting indictment of a system that makes it easier for someone to get arrested than to access effective addiction treatment. In a country that spends more per capita on incarceration than any other nation, the fact that jail could seem like a reasonable healthcare intervention reveals the depth of our policy failures around both criminal justice and public health.

The Sacramento County Jail, where Lopez served her sentence, was not designed to be a smoking cessation clinic, just as Deputy Campoy was not trained to be an addiction counselor. Yet both found themselves playing those roles in Lopez's unorthodox treatment plan. Her case suggests that until America develops more humane and accessible approaches to addiction treatment, the criminal justice system will continue to serve as a last resort for people who have exhausted all other options—even if it means committing a crime to access the help they need.
