## A 150-Year Saga of Colonial Removal and Indigenous Resilience

On a November day in 1868, two crew members from the British Royal Navy survey ship HMS *Topaze* stumbled upon something extraordinary while exploring the ceremonial village of Orongo on Easter Island. Buried up to its shoulders inside a stone house called Taura Renga sat a basalt moai—a statue unlike most others on the island[1][4]. The crew members had discovered Hoa Hakananai'a, a name that translates variously as "lost friend," "hidden friend," or most poignantly, "stolen friend"[1][4]. Within days, Commodore Richard Powell would make the decision that would forever separate this sacred statue from its homeland, setting in motion a controversy that continues to this day.

## The Sacred Sentinel of Orongo

Hoa Hakananai'a stands apart from Easter Island's approximately 900 moai statues in several significant ways[1]. Carved sometime between 1000 and 1200 AD, it is one of only fourteen moai made from basalt, a hard volcanic rock, rather than the softer volcanic tuff used for most of the island's famous sculptures[4][7][77]. At 2.42 meters (7.9 feet) tall and weighing 4.2 metric tons, it is relatively modest in size compared to some moai, yet it holds outsized spiritual and cultural significance[4][7].

The statue possesses the characteristic features of Easter Island moai: a heavy brow, blocky face with prominent nose and jutting chin, long rectangular ears, and thin arms angling down the sides with hands reaching toward the stomach[4]. The eyes were originally hollowed out and would have been inlaid with red stone and coral, and the sculpture was painted with red and white designs—decorations that were washed away when it was rafted to the British ship[7].

What makes Hoa Hakananai'a truly unique are the intricate petroglyphs carved on its back[1][4]. These low-relief designs are associated with the tangata manu, or birdman cult, that developed around 1400 AD—well after the statue itself was carved[1]. The statue appears to have had two distinct lives: first erected on an ahu-like ceremonial platform, and later moved into the stone house at Orongo, where its ritual function transformed to align with the emerging birdman religion[1][3].

## The Birdman Cult: A Spiritual Revolution

The ceremonial village of Orongo, perched on a narrow strip between the crater edge of the Rano Kau volcano and a 300-meter cliff plunging to the ocean, became the epicenter of a dramatic shift in Rapa Nui's spiritual and political landscape[1][9]. The birdman cult represented a departure from the ancestor worship embodied by the moai, emerging during a time of profound ecological and social change on the island.

Each spring, the arrival of the manutara—the sooty tern—to the offshore islets triggered the tangata manu competition[22][24][26]. Representatives called hopu, chosen by priests to represent different clans, would descend the sheer cliffs of Rano Kau, swim across dangerous shark-infested waters approximately 1.4 kilometers to Motu Nui islet, and wait for the birds to lay their first eggs[24][78][82]. The first to return with an intact egg would secure ritual authority for his patron for one year, fundamentally shifting power dynamics on the island[24][31].

![](/images/ZF4L-_Blprai_t7l00FM5.png)
<sub>Stone houses at Orongo ceremonial village on Easter Island linked to birdman competition</sub>

This competition was perilous. Many hopu died from shark attacks, drowning, or falls while climbing the treacherous cliffs[24][28]. Yet the stakes were enormous: in a society where traditional hereditary power structures had fractured, the tangata manu competition offered different clans the chance to rule[31]. The winner, considered sacred and embodying the creator god Make Make, lived in ceremonial seclusion for a year[22][24]. The last known competition took place around 1866 or 1867, just before Christian missionaries suppressed the practice[28][31][82].

![](/images/p8g_muUay1l7vRx5Q0rp8.png)
<sub>The Orongo ceremonial village crater on Easter Island, showing the unique landscape of Rano Kau crater surrounding the site</sub>

The petroglyphs on Hoa Hakananai'a's back bear witness to this spiritual transformation. The carvings include birdman figures, the M-shaped symbol representing the vulva and fertility, and circular designs connected to the cult's rituals[4][7]. The statue itself became a bridge between the old religion of ancestor worship and the new birdman cult, its body representing one era and its decorated back another[15].

## The Moai Factory: Rano Raraku

To understand what makes Hoa Hakananai'a exceptional, one must visit Rano Raraku, the volcanic crater that served as the quarry for approximately 95% of Easter Island's moai[37][39][47]. Here, in what Katherine Routledge—the British archaeologist who led the first true archaeological survey of the island in 1914—called "the nursery," some 397 moai remain in various stages of completion[37][94].

![](/images/ZK3dbW7DtevlQW4oLwokB.png)
<sub>Moai statues partially buried at Rano Raraku, the volcanic quarry on Easter Island where these iconic figures were carved</sub>

The sight is surreal: dozens of stone heads emerge from the grassy slopes, while hundreds of hollows and figures appear carved into the rocky surface of the mountain[37]. Some statues stand vertically with heads pointing upward, others are inverted, and still others lie horizontally, stacked in seemingly inaccessible positions[37]. The quarry operated for approximately 500 years until the early eighteenth century, during which time the moai grew increasingly larger and more refined[39][47].

The lapilli tuff at Rano Raraku was ideal for carving—softer than basalt but durable enough to create monumental sculptures[37][44]. Recent excavations have revealed that the quarry site also served as the island's most fertile agricultural zone, with evidence of sweet potato cultivation and other Polynesian crops from the 14th to 19th centuries[44]. The quarrying process itself created uniquely fertile soils from weathered tuff sediments, making Rano Raraku a multifunctional landscape unlike anywhere else on the island[44].

The tradition of moai construction reached its peak around 1500 AD, with some statues exceeding 10 meters in height and weighing 80 tons[39]. But this monumental achievement came at a devastating ecological cost. The transport of these massive sculptures required enormous amounts of timber, contributing to the complete deforestation of an island that had once been covered in palm forests[36][39][40].

## Ecological Collapse and Cultural Transformation

When Polynesian navigators first discovered and settled Rapa Nui around 1000 AD, using sophisticated wayfinding techniques that read stars, ocean swells, wind patterns, and even the flight paths of sooty terns, they encountered a forested island rich in resources[79][81]. The Easter Island palm, which provided building materials and fuel, dominated the landscape. Within several centuries, that forest would be gone[36][40].

The deforestation of Rapa Nui has been extensively debated among researchers, but recent evidence suggests a complex interplay of factors. Population pressure for agricultural land, particularly the development of stone mulching gardens after 1300 AD, drove extensive forest clearance[38]. The introduction of Polynesian rats, which consumed palm seeds, may have accelerated the process[36][40]. Climate variability, particularly droughts during the Little Ice Age, exacerbated crop productivity issues[38].

By the time European explorers arrived in 1722, the population had crashed from an estimated peak of 15,000 to just 2,000-3,000[36]. The island was almost completely deforested, and residents were malnourished[36]. Unable to build seaworthy vessels without large trees, the Rapa Nui could no longer fish effectively and shifted to eating birds and mollusks—until those resources, too, neared extinction[36].

The environmental collapse coincided with the toppling of moai. Around 1600 AD, statues began to be sporadically knocked down, evidence of the loss of confidence in the efficacy of ancestor worship[7]. This crisis created the conditions for the emergence of the birdman cult, which offered a new spiritual framework and a new basis for political authority[1][12].

## The HMS Topaze and the Taking of Hoa Hakananai'a

Into this transformed landscape sailed HMS *Topaze*, a 51-gun wooden screw frigate of the Royal Navy, in November 1868[21][23]. Under the command of Commodore Richard Ashmore Powell, the ship was conducting survey work in the Pacific. The crew had already removed one moai, Moai Hava, from the Mataveri area on November 2 when two crew members searching Orongo village discovered Hoa Hakananai'a[1][21].

![](/images/cjxLO1HZQ74pf7Smf69G7.png)
<sub>The HMS Topaze, a mid-19th century naval ship, displayed with signal flags in a historic black and white photograph</sub>

Powell decided to take this second statue as well. The stone house at Taura Renga was dismantled, and Hoa Hakananai'a was unearthed from where it stood buried up to its shoulders or more[4][21]. With picks, ropes, and timbers from the ship, approximately 50 men excavated the sculpture and dragged it three miles across rough ground on a sledge[23][29]. A Rapanui man named Tepano later recalled that the crew, followed by a Rapanui chief, dragged the moai down to the beach and floated it out to the ship on a raft made with empty casks[1][4]. The chief was so moved by the scene that he had it tattooed on his arm[1].

HMS *Topaze* arrived in Plymouth, England, on August 16, 1869[4][21]. Powell wrote to the British Admiralty offering both statues as gifts. The Admiralty, which until 1964 managed naval affairs, offered the statues to Queen Victoria[1][4][21]. The Queen accepted Hoa Hakananai'a and proposed that it should be given to the British Museum. On October 6, 1869, the arrival of the statue at the museum was officially reported to the Trustees[1]. Moai Hava was donated directly to the museum by the Admiralty[1][13].

## A Masterpiece in Exile

Hoa Hakananai'a was initially mounted on a plinth and exhibited outside the British Museum's front entrance, beneath the portico[4]. During World War II, it was taken inside for protection, where it mostly remained until 1966[4]. The statue then spent decades at the museum's Department of Ethnography at Burlington Gardens before returning to the main site in 2000[4][23]. Today, it stands on a high plinth in the Wellcome Trust Gallery (Room 24: Living and Dying), where it has been described as a "masterpiece" and among the finest examples of Easter Island sculpture[4][17].

In 2010, British Museum director Neil MacGregor selected Hoa Hakananai'a for his influential BBC Radio 4 series and book "A History of the World in 100 Objects"[4][15]. The choice brought renewed attention to the statue and, inevitably, to questions about its provenance and the ethics of its continued presence in London.

The British Museum has conducted extensive research on the statue. In 2012, a project using photogrammetry and polynomial texture mapping provided the first complete analysis of the statue's carvings[7]. This technological examination revealed details invisible to the naked eye, documenting the layered history inscribed on Hoa Hakananai'a's surface.

## The Fight for Return

Calls for the return of Hoa Hakananai'a are not new—they have persisted for decades—but they have intensified in recent years as international attitudes toward cultural repatriation have shifted[51][53]. In November 2018, a delegation from Rapa Nui traveled to London to request the statue's return[53][92]. The group included Tarita Alarcón Rapu, the governor of Easter Island, Felipe Ward, Chile's minister for national assets, and the Chilean ambassador to the UK[92][98].

The meeting marked the first time the British Museum had agreed to formal discussions about Hoa Hakananai'a since the statue entered its collection 150 years earlier[98]. When Governor Alarcón Rapu saw the moai, she was overwhelmed with emotion. "We are just a body," she told reporters through tears. "You, the British people, have our soul. And it is the right time to maybe send us back [the statue] for a while, so our sons can see it as I can see it. You have kept him for 150 years, just give us some months, and we can have it"[92][95][100].

The Rapa Nui delegation proposed several options, including a loan arrangement and the creation of a high-quality replica carved by island artisans to replace the original in the museum[53][98]. Indigenous sculptor Benedicto Tuki volunteered to create the replica for free[98]. British Museum officials accepted an invitation to visit Rapa Nui to continue discussions, but emphasized that they would only consider lending the statue, not permanent repatriation[53][92].

The museum's position rests on its role as a universal museum. A spokesperson stated there is "great value" in presenting objects from across the world and sharing stories of other cultures[98]. The museum noted that "the strength of the British Museum collection is its breadth and depth which allows millions of visitors an understanding of the cultures of the world and how they interconnect"[1].

In 2024, Chilean activists launched a social media campaign flooding the British Museum's Instagram with demands for the moai's return[13][60][65]. Digital influencer Mike Milfort, with over 1 million followers, rallied support and even received acknowledgment from Chilean President Gabriel Boric[60]. However, the campaign faced criticism from Rapa Nui Mayor Pedro Edmunds Paoa, who argued that it trivialized the moai by turning them into internet memes and disrespected their profound cultural significance[60].

## The Broader Context of Repatriation

Hoa Hakananai'a exists within a larger narrative of moai dispersed across the world. Approximately 10-12 moai were removed from Easter Island and now reside in museums in Paris, Washington D.C., Dunedin, Santiago, Brussels, and elsewhere[1][63]. In recent years, the Rapa Nui have initiated a coordinated repatriation effort called the Ka Haka Hoki Mai Te Mana Tupuna Repatriation Program[57].

In 2022, one moai that had been held at Chile's National Museum of Natural History in Santiago for over a century was successfully returned to Easter Island[54][57]. The 715-kilogram sculpture was transported by truck to Valparaíso and then by naval ship on a five-day journey to Rapa Nui[54]. "For the first time, a moai will return to the island from the mainland," announced Chile's Minister of Culture Consuelo Valdés[54][57].

The return was celebrated with traditional ceremonies at the Hanga Piko docks, and the moai was housed at the Padre Sebastián Englert Anthropological Museum[57]. "What has happened is so important for us that I've smiled all day," said Anakena Manutomatoma, president of the CODEIPA Development Subcommittee[57].

For the Rapa Nui, these repatriations are about more than physical objects. The moai are considered aringa ora—the living faces of ancestors[1][57]. They are believed to contain mana, ancestral energy needed to protect the islanders[57]. The thinking is that should the moai return, they would bring with them their lost mana and ability to protect Rapa Nui[57].

## The Island Today

Easter Island was annexed by Chile in 1888 through the "Treaty of Annexation," signed between Chilean representative Policarpo Toro and Atamu Tekena, designated "King" by the Chilean government[93][96]. The validity of this treaty remains contested by some Rapa Nui[96]. For decades, the indigenous inhabitants were confined to a small portion of the island while the Williamson-Balfour Company leased the rest as a sheep farm[57][93].

In 2017, the Rapa Nui gained self-administration over their ancestral lands from Chile[51]. In December of that year, the indigenous Rapa Nui people took over the task of conserving, preserving, and managing their archaeological heritage[92]. Chilean law now holds that the moai are not mere objects but an integral part of the land itself[92].

Today, the island faces new challenges. Climate change threatens the moai with erosion and rising seas[66]. The population has rebounded from a low of 111 in 1877 to approximately 8,600 people, but tourism and environmental pressures create ongoing management issues[36]. The Ma'u Henua Indigenous Community works to conserve the moai as a blueprint for future monument preservation[66].

Meanwhile, the sooty tern—the manutara whose eggs once held such sacred significance—sadly no longer migrates to Easter Island due to habitat loss and the introduction of exotic species[31][90]. In December 2022, approximately 300 nesting pairs arrived on neighboring Motu Motiro Hiva, raising hope that this culturally important bird could one day return[31].

## The Meaning of a Masterpiece

Cristian Arevalo Pakarati, a Rapa Nui scholar who has created over 4,000 drawings for the Easter Island Statue Project, wrote eloquently about what Hoa Hakananai'a represents[15]. Having worked with the statues for years, seeing them in different contexts and perspectives—some standing, some toppled from their ancestral pedestals—he views Hoa Hakananai'a as unique among equals.

"Our 'hidden friend,' the moai Hoa Hakananai'a is all that, and accomplishes the idea of showing changes—the old, the present, and the future," Pakarati wrote[15]. "He could easily belong to any era. He was re-carved with new symbols on his back to match the requirement of a specific new situation—the coming of new ideas and beliefs with changes of politics and religion. Maybe that was his real message—to last"[15].

Pakarati sees Hoa Hakananai'a as "the perfect representation of those changes, and the ability of humans to adapt in order to survive"[15]. He calls the statue "the best ambassador for Easter Island—the one who is really going to last and to transcend all time"[15].

Yet this ambassador has been separated from his homeland for more than 150 years. Whether Hoa Hakananai'a will return to Rapa Nui remains uncertain. The conversations between the British Museum and the Rapa Nui community continue, set against an evolving international landscape where questions of cultural patrimony, colonial legacy, and indigenous rights are being reexamined with increasing urgency.

For the Rapa Nui people, the moai are not artifacts to be studied or displayed. They are ancestors, spirits, and sources of protection. As Governor Alarcón Rapu's tears outside the British Museum demonstrated, the separation is not merely geographic but spiritual. "You have our soul," she said[92][100]. In those four words lies the heart of the matter—a question not just of where Hoa Hakananai'a belongs, but of whose story he is ultimately meant to tell.
