## When Human Beings Became Cooling Machines, and Deafness Became Desirable

In the sweltering heat of colonial India, where temperatures soared past 40°C and European bodies wilted in the suffocating humidity, there existed a peculiar solution: the punkah. It was not a technological marvel, nor an invention of Western ingenuity. Instead, it was a large cloth-and-cane panel suspended from the ceiling, rhythmically swayed back and forth by human hands—or, more accurately, human feet—to circulate air through the colonizers' homes, offices, and barracks. At first glance, it seems almost quaint, a low-tech response to a very real problem. But behind every gentle breeze that cooled the brow of a British administrator, every comfortable night's sleep in an Indian bungalow, lay a dark reality: the systematic exploitation of India's poorest citizens, and a troubling detail that reveals the invasiveness of colonial power—a preference for deaf servants.

This is the story of the punkah-wallahs, whose bodies became instruments of imperial comfort and whose labor was so thoroughly devalued that colonial records barely bothered to record their names.

## The Origins of a Cooling Revolution

The punkah itself was not a British invention. Its origins trace back to the Arab world, where it was believed to have been developed around the time of Caliph Mansur in the Islamic Golden Age, originally known as *Marwaha al-Khaish*—the linen fan. From the Middle East, it traveled eastward through Persian territories and eventually reached the courts of the Mughal Empire, where records suggest it was already being used by the time of Emperor Shah Jahan (r. 1628–58). There are even accounts of mechanized, steam-powered punkahs appearing in some early nineteenth-century courts of Awadh in northern India, suggesting that technological experimentation with the device was already underway long before the British encountered it.

When the British East India Company established its hold over India, they discovered a heat-management system already embedded in the subcontinent's cultural and practical landscape. The Company was quick to adopt it—not because they invented it, but because it offered them something precious: a way to remain comfortable, and therefore productive, in an unbearably hot climate. Without the punkah, one contemporary observer suggested, colonialism itself might have moved at a different pace. As one historian notes, the British simply could not have conducted their affairs of state with the same efficiency had they been perpetually sleep-deprived and heat-exhausted.

By the 1840s, the punkah had become, in the words of colonial observers, an "indispensable" fixture in "all principal apartments" of a European home—bedrooms, dining rooms, even bathrooms. The British army, recognizing the device's importance to maintaining soldier health and morale, began officially subsidizing the cost of punkahs and their operators. From 1848 onward, the imperial state bore the expense of installing and operating these fans in every barracks, courthouse, hospital, church, and office across India. What had once been a luxury for the wealthy elite was now a standard feature of colonial infrastructure.

## The Men Behind the Motion: An Invisible Workforce

With the punkah came an entirely new occupational category: the punkah-wallah, or punkah coolie. These were not skilled craftsmen or educated servants; they were drawn from the lowest castes and social strata of Indian society—sweepers, palanquin-bearers, and other groups already positioned at the bottom of the domestic servant hierarchy. They were men (and in rare cases, women) without social standing, without protection, and without meaningful recourse when mistreated.

Initially, punkah-wallahs worked only during the hot months, from March through October, sitting in one corner of the room they were cooling, rope in hand, engaged in the monotonous work of maintaining air circulation. But as the practice became normalized and the demand for comfort intensified, the role evolved. The punkah extended into the evenings and nights. Employers, desperate for uninterrupted sleep in the oppressive heat, demanded that their coolies maintain the fan's motion through the darkness. And as the punkah moved into more private spaces—bedrooms, bathrooms—the architecture of colonial homes was literally redesigned around the desire to hide these servants from view.

The rope threading through a hole in the wall became standard. The punkah-wallah, now stationed outside the room, became what historians call a "liminal" figure—neither fully inside the household nor outside it, existing in the margins of colonial domestic life. They became, in essence, invisible. This architectural banishment served a dual purpose: it hid from the colonizers the physical bodies of servants they found uncomfortable to look at for extended periods, and it solved what colonists saw as a growing problem of poor work conditions.

## The Machinery of Exploitation

The work itself was grueling. A punkah-wallah would pull the rope in steady, repetitive motions, hour after hour, sometimes using their feet instead of their hands, lying on their backs as they worked. Shifts lasted through the night, and wages were appallingly low—far lower than other forms of domestic service. The colonial government's concern with keeping costs down meant that it resisted modernization even when alternatives became available. Early mechanical innovations, such as treadle-based machines that could do the work of multiple men, were developed by British engineers. One such device, invented by engineer Grant, could apparently perform the work of twelve coolies operated by just one man. Yet these were never widely adopted. Cheaper labor, it seemed, was preferable to technological progress.

Many punkah-wallahs tried to survive by working flexible shifts or taking employment with multiple households simultaneously—a small act of agency in an otherwise powerless situation. But European employers viewed this flexibility with suspicion and hostility, interpreting it as laziness or disloyalty. Their primary concern was the one fear that colonized the imaginations of every punkah-pulling household: that the servant would fall asleep.

Falling asleep was, of course, inevitable. A man working through the night, often deliberately kept in uncomfortable conditions to prevent drowsiness, pushing or pulling a rope for hours, naturally would doze. And when he did, the consequences were swift and brutal.

## Punishment, Violence, and the Sleep of Empire

Colonial documents and personal accounts reveal a landscape of casual, systematic violence inflicted on punkah-wallahs. Masters kept "missiles" on hand—boots, slippers, racquets, even chairs—to hurl at a dozing servant. Some placed the coolie on a high stool, hoping he would tumble off if he lost consciousness. Others employed more inventive torments: one planter would sprinkle sugar around the spot where the punkah-wallah sat, allowing ants to bite and torment his sweaty skin. Another would place a live duck or goose beneath a sleeping coolie's arms, punishing the servant if the bird escaped. British officer George Atkinson wrote with shocking casualness about these practices, describing them as simple necessities of household management.

But some punishments crossed from torment into brutality. Kicking and beating were common. One master confessed to "rising six times in a night to kick his punkah-bearer awake," submitting himself, he complained, to "a life of compulsory hypochondria and inevitable valetudinarianism." Jars of water—kept by bedsides ostensibly for drinking—were often reserved for throwing at servants. Some colonists broke earthen pots filled with water on the bodies of sleeping coolies.

And sometimes, the violence turned fatal.

By the final decades of the nineteenth century, newspapers in India began reporting cases where punkah-wallahs died from the injuries inflicted by their employers. The case of Tulsia, a punkah coolie who died from a blow struck by Mr. Fox, the Assistant Station-Master of Tundla, in August 1879, reached the courts. The Allahabad High Court ruled that Fox had not committed murder—indeed, that he had acted naturally, almost reflexively, in response to the laziness of a man who had fallen asleep. The court essentially declared that it was reasonable to beat a man to death if he failed to keep fanning you.

Over the three decades following this verdict, twenty-one cases were documented from army barracks alone where punkah-wallahs died from violence inflicted by European soldiers. Nearly all were dismissed with similar judicial reasoning. Some cases never even reached trial, covered up by military authorities who simply declared them accidents or claimed they could not identify the perpetrators. Colonial records were far more meticulous about documenting the costs of replacing a broken punkah than about investigating the deaths of the men who operated them.

## The Privacy Paradox: When Deafness Became Desirable

Here is where the story takes a turn toward the particularly troubling: as the punkah became a fixture of private life—bedrooms, bathrooms, intimate spaces—colonists began to worry about something other than cost or maintenance. They were concerned about privacy. These servants, necessarily present while their employers slept, bathed, and discussed confidential matters, could hear everything. They could listen to secrets, overhear business dealings, witness indiscretions. They posed a threat not to physical comfort, but to the illusion of privacy that colonists wished to maintain.

The solution, some colonists discovered, was to employ deaf punkah-wallahs.

A deaf servant could not listen. He could not report what he heard. He could not be accused of knowing things he shouldn't know. More importantly, his deafness allowed colonists to maintain a psychological fiction: that he was not truly present, that his labour was somehow less invasive because his senses were diminished. A man who could see but not hear was transformed, in the colonist's mind, from a human being witnessing their private moments into something closer to a machine.

This preference for deaf servants reveals something profound about colonial mentality. Rather than reconsidering the invasiveness of having a servant present during intimate moments—rather than accepting that this was the price of their chosen system—colonists simply selected workers whose disabilities made them seem less like observers and more like tools. It was not enough that punkah-wallahs were underpaid, overworked, and brutalized. They had to be selected, where possible, to be incapable of bearing witness.

## The Invisible Network of Colonial Power

What the punkah system shows us is that colonialism was not sustained by technology or military might alone, but by the systematic exploitation of the most vulnerable members of society. Every comfort of the colonizer was built on the discomfort of the colonized. Every hour of sleep purchased with a few coins came from the sleeplessness of a man held awake by fear or ants or the possibility of violence. The punkah itself—that gentle, almost poetic solution to the problem of heat—was in reality a mechanism for extracting labor from the powerless.

The punkah-wallahs had remarkably little recourse. Their low caste status meant that other servants would not work alongside them, isolating them further. Their economic desperation meant they could not refuse. The occasional strikes—like one documented in 1898—were rare and largely unsuccessful. By law, they had virtually no protection. The judicial system, from the highest courts down, operated on the assumption that their lives and wellbeing were worth far less than the comfort of their employers.

The professions of punkah-wallah would not disappear until technology finally intervened—not through the mechanical innovations that colonists had resisted, but through the arrival of electric fans in the late 1890s and early 1900s. As electricity gradually became available and electric fans became affordable, the demand for human-powered cooling evaporated. By the 1940s, the punkah-wallah had become a memory. No longer necessary, these workers dispersed, finding other forms of servile work or returning to whatever lives they could cobble together in a colonized country.

## A Footnote to History

There are no monuments to the punkah-wallahs. No memorials acknowledge their collective suffering. Their names are largely unrecorded. What remains are scattered mentions in colonial diaries, dismissive references in official reports, and the photographs—frozen moments where a dark-skinned man pulls a rope while pale-skinned colonists enjoy their comfort beneath it. These images, often presented as picturesque glimpses of "exotic" colonial life, reveal far more than their makers intended: the routine nature of the exploitation, the casualness with which human beings were reduced to labor, the deep inequality that made such arrangements possible.

The punkah reminds us that comfort, especially colonial comfort, always has a human cost. And the preference for deaf punkah-wallahs reminds us that when systems of exploitation become normalized, people find ways to make them psychologically bearable—even when those ways involve selecting for the disabilities of the exploited.

Today, when we think of technological progress in colonized societies, we often focus on railways and telegraphs, on the infrastructure that colonizers built. But the true infrastructure of colonialism was human—built from the bodies and labor of people deemed worth less than the shade they provided.

---

*The punkah-wallah profession has faded into history, but the dynamics it reveals—the devaluation of labor, the exploitation of the vulnerable, the ways that systems of oppression become normal and invisible—remain deeply relevant. As we examine the hidden histories of colonialism, the story of these servants reminds us that behind every comfort and convenience lies someone else's work, and someone else's sacrifice.*
