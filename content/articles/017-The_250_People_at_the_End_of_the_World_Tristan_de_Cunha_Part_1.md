![The volcanic island of Tristan da Cunha rising from the South Atlantic Ocean, with Queen Mary's Peak shrouded in cloud](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/78/Tristan_da_Cunha%2C_British_overseas_territory-20March2012.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

Pull up a map. Any map. Find South America on the left, Africa on the right, and then look at the vast, empty blue of the South Atlantic Ocean in between. There's nothing there. Thousands upon thousands of kilometres of open water, wind, and waves.

Now look closer. Somewhere roughly midway between Buenos Aires and Cape Town — 2,400 kilometres from Africa, 3,300 from South America — there's a dot. A volcanic speck that most maps don't even bother to label. That dot is Tristan da Cunha, and around 220 people call it home.

![Map showing Tristan da Cunha's location in the vast emptiness of the South Atlantic Ocean.](<https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b2/Tristan_da_Cunha_on_the_Globe_(in_the_United_Kingdom).svg>)

There is no airport. There is no runway. The only way to get there is a six-day boat ride from Cape Town, aboard one of the handful of supply ships that visit fewer than a dozen times per year. If you somehow get there, you'll find a single settlement — Edinburgh of the Seven Seas, a name with arguably more letters than the town has buildings — huddled on the one sliver of flat land at the base of a 2,062-metre volcano. There are no roads leading out, because there is nowhere for them to go.

And if you fall in love with the place? Tough luck. You can't move here. The island doesn't accept new residents. The community is full, and has been for a while.

This is the story of how it got that way.

![Edinburgh of the Seven Seas — the world's most remote settlement, huddled at the base of a volcano.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/55/The_Main_Settlement_on_Tristan_da_Cunha_-_Flickr_-_The_Official_CTBTO_Photostream.jpg)

## A Rock Named After a Man Who Never Stepped on It

In May 1506, a Portuguese admiral named Tristão da Cunha was sailing toward India when he spotted a cluster of volcanic islands jutting out of the South Atlantic. Rough seas prevented him from landing, so he did the next best thing — he named the biggest one after himself and sailed on.

For the next three centuries, that was pretty much the extent of Tristan da Cunha's relationship with the rest of humanity. The Dutch stopped by a few times in the 1600s, considered setting up a supply base, and thought better of it — mostly because there was no safe place to anchor a ship. The British East India Company had the same idea and reached the same conclusion. The island had cliffs, wind, and absolutely no hospitality for visitors. It was, by all accounts, a rock with an attitude problem.

Ships occasionally stopped for fresh water. Whalers sheltered in the lee of the island when the Atlantic got mean. But nobody stayed.

Until an American with a flair for the dramatic decided he'd had enough of civilisation.

## The Self-Proclaimed King of Refreshment

In December 1810, a man named Jonathan Lambert — "late of Salem, mariner and citizen thereof," as he liked to style himself — rowed ashore on Tristan with two companions and declared the island his personal kingdom. He renamed the entire archipelago the "Islands of Refreshment" (genuinely excellent branding, honestly) and announced his sovereignty to the world, "grounding my right and claim on the rational and sure ground of absolute occupancy."

In other words: nobody else was here, so it's mine.

Lambert's reign was short. He and one of his companions drowned while fishing in May 1812, barely eighteen months after planting his flag. The third man, an Italian named Tomasso Corri, was left alone on the island — a castaway in a kingdom of one — until a British ship rescued him two years later.

The Islands of Refreshment were once again empty.

But history was already cooking up the event that would change everything, and it had to do with a short, exiled Frenchman on a neighbouring island.

## Napoleon's Unlikely Gift

When Napoleon Bonaparte was packed off to his final exile on St. Helena in 1815, the British got nervous. St. Helena was only about 2,500 kilometres north of Tristan da Cunha, and the Admiralty worried that someone might use the closer island as a staging ground for a rescue attempt. So in 1816, they did what empires do — they sent a garrison.

Five officers and thirty-six soldiers arrived from South Africa and formally claimed the island for Britain. They built some structures, raised a flag, and settled in to guard against a French threat that, in hindsight, was never really coming.

A year later, the threat officially passed, the garrison was recalled — and the story of Tristan da Cunha truly began.

Because one man refused to leave.

## Corporal Glass and "The Firm"

His name was William Glass. Born William Glasgow in Kelso, Scotland in 1786, he'd joined the British Army as a teenager (shaving two years off his age and quietly shortening his surname in the process — a mystery that has never been fully explained). By the time the garrison was pulled from Tristan in 1817, Glass was a corporal with a wife, Maria Magdalena Leenders — a woman of mixed African, Asian, and European descent from the Cape Colony — and two small children.

Glass looked at the empty volcanic island and saw something nobody else did: home.

He requested permission to stay. The commander agreed, and even left behind a bull, a cow, and a few sheep as a parting gift. Two stonemasons from Plymouth, Samuel Burnell and John Nankivel, also chose to remain. And together, on 7 November 1817, these three men signed a document that would shape the next two centuries of life on this rock.

They called their agreement "The Firm."

It read, in part, that "the stock and stores of every description" would be shared equally, that "whatever profit may arise shall be equally divided," and — here's the kicker — that "no member shall assume any superiority whatever, but all to be considered as equal in every respect."

Let that sink in. In 1817 — decades before Marx, over a century before any communist revolution — three men on a dot in the Atlantic wrote up a founding charter based on radical equality, collective ownership, and shared labour. No leaders. No hierarchy. No private property.

A Scottish corporal with a mysterious name change. A South African wife. Two Plymouth stonemasons. A bull, a cow, a few sheep. And a handshake that said: _we're all equal here._

That was the beginning.

![The founding agreement of 'The Firm,' signed in 1817, promised absolute equality among Tristan's first settlers.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b8/H.M._late_sloop_Julia_off_Tristan_da_Cunha_RMG_PW8555.jpg)

## Five Brides and a Bag of Potatoes

The community grew — slowly, and in the most improbable ways.

A few English sailors joined in the early years. Richard Riley was shipwrecked on the sealer _Emily_ in 1820 and decided he liked the place. Alexander Cotton arrived aboard HMS _Satellite_ the same year and never left. Settlers came and went, but the core group held.

There was, however, a problem. By the mid-1820s, Tristan was an island of bachelors. And bachelors — no matter how committed they are to communal potato farming — do not make a self-sustaining colony.

So the men did something wonderfully practical: they asked a passing naval captain if he could pick them up some wives.

The request made its way to St. Helena, where five women — of mixed African, Asian, and European descent — apparently agreed to make the 2,500-kilometre journey to marry men they had never met, on an island they had never seen. According to one popular account, the captain's payment was to be one bag of potatoes per bride.

In 1827, the women arrived. And just like that, the community began to multiply.

By 1832, there were six couples and twenty-two children on Tristan. The little colony was finding its feet. Families cultivated potato patches on the slopes above the settlement, raised cattle and sheep, and bartered fresh vegetables and water with passing ships in exchange for flour, sugar, and tea. There was no currency — the potato was the unit of exchange. Life was simple, hard, and theirs.

![The Potato Patches — walled agricultural plots where Tristan islanders have grown their food for over 160 years.](https://www.mygrations.nl/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Tristan1-1140x700-1.jpg)

## The Seven Names

What makes Tristan truly extraordinary is that everyone who lives there today can trace their ancestry back to an absurdly small group of founders. Over the course of the 19th century, exactly seven surnames took root on the island — each one a thread in the story:

**Glass** — William, the Scottish corporal. The founding father.

**Swain** — Thomas Swain, from Hastings, England. One of the early arrivals.

**Green** — Peter Groen, a Dutchman who showed up in 1836 and wisely anglicised his surname for an English-speaking island.

**Rogers** — Thomas Rogers, an American whaler who settled in 1837.

**Hagan** — Andrew Hagan, another American whaler, arriving in 1849.

**Repetto** and **Lavarello** — But these two? They came later, and their arrival is one of the most dramatic chapters in the island's story. We'll get there.

For now, picture this: an entire society built from five surnames, a handful of families, and a potato-based economy. No outside authority. No police. No written laws. Just "The Firm" and the understanding that on a rock this small, you either cooperate or you don't survive.

The Tristan phonebook — if such a thing existed — would have been one page long. And because the same combinations of first names and surnames kept repeating, the islanders developed an elaborate system of nicknames. Nearly everyone got one, usually commemorating a particular event in their life or a personality quirk. When three people in your village are named William Green, you need a better system than alphabetical order.

## The Invisible Inheritance

Here's where the story takes a turn that a geneticist would find electrifying.

When you build an entire population from fifteen founders — eight men and seven women, arriving between 1816 and 1908 — something strange happens to the gene pool. Traits that might be vanishingly rare in a large population get amplified. Quirks of biology that would disappear into the statistical noise of London or Cape Town get passed down, over and over, in a community where second cousins marrying is not just common but mathematically unavoidable.

On Tristan, the most visible consequence is asthma.

About 36% of the island's population has it — a staggeringly high rate by any global standard. Researchers from the University of Toronto have been studying the islanders since the 1990s, and their findings point to a classic "founder effect": historical records suggest that William Glass and his wife Maria were both asthmatic. That one couple's genetic contribution has rippled across two centuries and the entire population. When nearly everyone on your island is descended from the same fifteen people, one couple's lungs can shape a community's medical reality for generations.

The islanders have become one of the most studied populations in human genetics — not because anything is "wrong" with them, but because their tiny, well-documented family tree offers scientists a living laboratory for understanding how genes move through generations. Researchers have traced the Y chromosomes of present-day Tristanians back to the exact founding fathers. They've mapped mitochondrial DNA to five specific founding mothers. It's a family tree with actual roots you can name.

## The Darkest Day

By the 1880s, life on Tristan had settled into a rhythm that was harsh but familiar. The Suez Canal had opened in 1869, rerouting global shipping away from the South Atlantic. Sailing ships gave way to steamships. The island that once saw regular traffic from whalers and East Indiamen was now genuinely, deeply alone.

And then came the worst day in the island's history.

On 27 November 1885, the iron barque _West Riding_ appeared on the horizon, heading from Bristol to Sydney. This was a rare and precious thing — a passing ship meant a chance to trade, to get flour and provisions in exchange for potatoes and livestock. Almost every able-bodied man on the island scrambled into the community's lifeboat and rowed out to meet her.

Fifteen men in one boat.

The lifeboat was spotted alongside the _West Riding_ for a time, then lost from sight.

It never came back.

Fifteen men — virtually every working-age male on the island — vanished. No wreckage was ever found. No explanation was ever confirmed. Some speculated they drowned in the heavy seas. Others whispered that they'd been taken aboard and sold as slaves in Australia. The truth has never been established, and the mystery still haunts the community today.

What is known is what was left behind.

Out of a population of ninety-two, there were now only four adult males — and one of them had lost his mind. Tristan da Cunha, this tiny settlement built on equality and cooperation, became an island of widows and fatherless children.

It is hard to overstate the scale of this loss. Imagine a village where every family loses its father, husband, or brother in a single afternoon. Now imagine that village is the most isolated place on Earth, with no phone, no radio, and no guarantee that anyone would come to help.

The women ran the island. They tended the potato patches, managed the livestock, raised the children, and kept The Firm alive. The British government — stirred by reports from a returning clergyman (who happened to be the youngest brother of Lewis Carroll, the author of _Alice in Wonderland_) — sent supplies and offered to evacuate everyone to Cape Town.

The islanders refused. Most of them chose to stay.

## Salvation from the Sea

For years, the community held on — diminished, grieving, but stubborn. And then, as if the ocean felt it owed Tristan a debt, the sea brought new blood.

In 1892, the Italian ship _Italia_ wrecked off the coast. Among the crew were two sailors from the town of Camogli on the Italian Riviera: Andrea Repetto and Gaetano Lavarello. Both men decided to stay on the island. Both married local women.

They brought something the island desperately needed — not just labour, but skills. The Italians were experienced craftsmen, and their carpentry helped design and build the fleet of sturdy longboats that Tristan would depend on for generations. To this day, the Tristan longboat is an icon of island life, and it was born from Italian hands and Atlantic necessity.

![A Tristan da Cunha longboat — the design pioneered by Italian shipwreck survivors in the 1890s, still in use today.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4f/Tristan_da_Cunha_-_Lobster_longboat.jpg/960px-Tristan_da_Cunha_-_Lobster_longboat.jpg)

With Repetto and Lavarello, the seventh and final founding surnames were in place. Seven names. Seven families. A phonebook with one page and a gene pool you could map on a napkin.

Glass, Swain, Green, Rogers, Hagan, Repetto, Lavarello.

That was the roster, and it would remain essentially unchanged for the next 130 years.

---

By the dawn of the 20th century, Tristan da Cunha had survived everything the world and the ocean could throw at it: imperial neglect, starvation, the loss of nearly all its men, shipwrecks, and the slow erasure of the island from the world's shipping lanes. Its people had built a society on a volcanic rock with no harbour, no roads, no currency, and no connection to the outside world that couldn't be severed by a bad stretch of weather.

They had their potato patches, their longboats, their seven surnames, and a two-hundred-year-old promise that no one would be above anyone else.

They didn't know it yet, but the biggest test was still coming. The mountain they lived beneath — the 2,062-metre volcano they called Queen Mary's Peak — had been asleep for centuries.

In 1961, it woke up.
